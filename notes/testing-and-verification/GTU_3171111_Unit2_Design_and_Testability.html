SECTION 01

Introduction to Design and Testability

Definition

Design and Testability is the discipline of building a circuit so that its internal nodes can be easily controlled and observed for test purposes, treating testability as a design goal alongside area, speed, and power — not as an afterthought handled once the chip already exists.

A circuit designed with no thought for testing might be functionally perfect yet nearly untestable — its internal nodes may be so deeply buried that generating test patterns takes impossibly long, or some faults may never be detectable at all. "Design and Testability" flips the responsibility: testability becomes a design-time decision.

Key Point / Remember

The guiding principle of this unit is: "Design for Testability first, generate tests second." A testable design makes ATPG (Unit 3) fast and gives near-complete fault coverage; an untestable design makes ATPG slow, incomplete, or impossible.

Real-World Example

Think of a car engine. A well-designed engine bay has access panels and diagnostic ports built in at design time, so a mechanic can quickly plug in a scanner and read every sensor. A poorly designed one might require removing the entire engine just to check one sensor. DFT is the chip equivalent of designing those access panels in from day one.

SECTION 02

Testability Analysis

Definition

Testability analysis is a set of heuristic (approximate, fast-to-compute) techniques used to estimate how hard it is to control and observe every node in a circuit, without actually running full ATPG. The most widely taught method is SCOAP (Sandia Controllability/Observability Analysis Program).

SCOAP assigns every node two controllability numbers and one observability number:

  • CC0 — combinational difficulty of setting the node to logic 0.
  • CC1 — combinational difficulty of setting the node to logic 1.
  • CO — combinational difficulty of observing the node at a primary output.
Key Point / Remember

SCOAP numbers measure difficulty, not ease — a higher number means the node is harder to control/observe. Primary inputs always start at the minimum value, CC0 = CC1 = 1.

Real-World Example

Think of CC0/CC1 like a "number of steps needed" score to reach a hidden light switch behind furniture. A switch right next to the door has a score of 1 (easy); one behind three stacked boxes in a locked closet has a much higher score. SCOAP is exactly this — a step-count estimate, not an exact test pattern.

Table 2.2 — Basic SCOAP Combinational Controllability Rules
GateCC1 (output)CC0 (output)
Primary Input11
AND (a, b)CC1(a) + CC1(b) + 1min(CC0(a), CC0(b)) + 1
OR (a, b)min(CC1(a), CC1(b)) + 1CC0(a) + CC0(b) + 1
NOT (a)CC0(a) + 1CC1(a) + 1
Worked Numerical Example — SCOAP Controllability

Circuit: Primary inputs A and B feed a 2-input AND gate G1. The output of G1 feeds a NOT gate G2, whose output is Z.

Step 1 — Primary inputs: CC0(A) = CC1(A) = 1; CC0(B) = CC1(B) = 1 (by definition).

Step 2 — AND gate G1:

CC1(G1) = CC1(A) + CC1(B) + 1 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3

CC0(G1) = min(CC0(A), CC0(B)) + 1 = min(1,1) + 1 = 2

Step 3 — NOT gate G2 (output Z):

CC0(Z) = CC1(G1) + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4

CC1(Z) = CC0(G1) + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3

Result: Node Z has CC0 = 4, CC1 = 3 — meaning it takes a few more logical "steps" to force Z to 0 than to 1, information a test engineer can use to flag Z as a relatively hard-to-control node worth adding a test point near.

Key Point / Remember

Sequential SCOAP measures (SC0, SC1, SO) work the same way but also count the number of clock cycles needed to propagate through flip-flops — useful for spotting nodes that are hard to reach purely because they sit deep inside sequential logic.

SECTION 03

Design for Testability Basics

Definition

Design for Testability (DFT) is the collection of design techniques that add extra hardware or design discipline to a circuit purely to improve its controllability and observability, at the cost of some silicon area, timing, and power overhead.

DFT techniques fall into two broad families:

  • Ad-hoc DFT: Localized, experience-based fixes — inserting test points at specific hard-to-test nodes identified via testability analysis (e.g., SCOAP), without any global structural rule.
  • Structured DFT: A systematic, uniform methodology applied across the entire chip — the most common example being scan design, where every flip-flop is replaced by a scan flip-flop following strict rules (Sections 4–6 of this unit).
Key Point / Remember

Structured DFT (scan) is preferred in modern industry flows because it is automatable — EDA tools can insert it and generate patterns for it without manual intervention, which ad-hoc DFT cannot guarantee.

Real-World Example

Ad-hoc DFT is like patching a single squeaky floorboard you noticed while walking through a house. Structured DFT is like installing a uniform grid of access panels under every floorboard in the house at construction time — more upfront cost, but guaranteed access everywhere, and any future inspector knows exactly where to look.

Table 2.3 — Ad-hoc DFT vs. Structured DFT
AspectAd-hoc DFTStructured DFT (Scan-based)

Worked Numerical Example — Area Overhead of DFT

Given: A design has 50,000 gate-equivalents (GE) before DFT. Adding scan logic (extra MUX per flip-flop) adds 1,500 GE.

Area overhead % = (Added GE / Original GE) × 100

= (1,500 / 50,000) × 100 = 3%

Interpretation: A 3% area overhead is a typical, acceptable price to pay for the dramatic increase in fault coverage and automatability that structured scan provides.

SECTION 04

Scan Cell Designs

Definition

A scan cell is a modified flip-flop or latch that can operate in two modes: normal (functional) mode, behaving exactly like the original storage element, and scan (shift) mode, where it becomes one link of a shift-register chain used to load and unload test data.

Fig 2.1 — Mux-D scan cell: a 2:1 MUX selects functional data (SE=0) or scan-in data (SE=1) into the flip-flop

4.1Common scan cell types

  • Mux-D scan cell: A 2:1 multiplexer placed before a standard D flip-flop, controlled by a Scan-Enable (SE) signal. Simplest and most widely used in industry.
  • Clocked-scan cell: Uses a separate scan clock pin instead of a mux, switching between functional and scan clocks — reduces mux delay but needs extra clock routing.
  • LSSD (Level Sensitive Scan Design) cell: Uses two level-sensitive latches (master + slave) driven by non-overlapping clocks, guaranteeing race-free, level-sensitive operation regardless of clock edge rates.
Fig 2.2 — LSSD double-latch scan cell structure
Key Point / Remember

Mux-D is edge-triggered and needs only 1 extra clock (shared scan clock); LSSD is level-sensitive and needs multiple non-overlapping clocks but is inherently race-free — a classic speed-vs-robustness trade-off.

Real-World Example

A Mux-D cell is like a railway point/switch: one lever (SE) decides whether the train (data) goes on the "normal" or "test" track through the same station. LSSD is more like a two-stage airlock (master then slave chamber) where each door opens only when the other is sealed shut — slower to pass through, but nothing can leak through by accident.

Table 2.4 — Mux-D Scan Cell vs. LSSD Scan Cell
AspectMux-D Scan CellLSSD Scan Cell
Trigger typeEdge-triggeredLevel-sensitive (double latch)
Clocking1 functional clock + shared scan enableMultiple non-overlapping clocks
Race conditionsPossible if clock skew is poorInherently race-free by design
Area/delay overheadLower (just 1 mux)Higher (extra latch + clock routing)
Industry usageVery common (default in most ASIC flows)Historically used in IBM mainframes; less common now
Worked Numerical Example — Overhead Comparison

Given: A design has 10,000 flip-flops. Assume each Mux-D scan cell adds ~4 gate-equivalents (GE), while each LSSD cell adds ~8 GE (extra latch + clocking). Base combinational logic = 200,000 GE.

Mux-D total added: 10,000 × 4 = 40,000 GE → overhead = 40,000 / 200,000 × 100 = 20%

LSSD total added: 10,000 × 8 = 80,000 GE → overhead = 80,000 / 200,000 × 100 = 40%

Conclusion: Mux-D scan roughly halves the area overhead compared to LSSD for the same number of flip-flops — a major reason it dominates modern ASIC design.

SECTION 05

Scan Architectures

Definition

A scan architecture defines how scan cells are organized and connected at the chip level — how many chains exist, which flip-flops belong to each chain, and whether scan is applied only internally or also at the chip's I/O boundary.

5.1Major architecture types

  • Full scan: Every flip-flop in the design is converted to a scan cell — gives the highest fault coverage since the whole sequential circuit effectively becomes combinational-like for test purposes.
  • Partial scan: Only a selected subset of flip-flops (chosen via testability analysis) is converted — reduces area/timing overhead at the cost of some fault coverage.
  • Multiple (parallel) scan chains: Instead of one very long chain, flip-flops are split into several shorter chains operating in parallel, cutting test time.
  • Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1 / JTAG): Scan cells placed specifically at the chip's I/O pins, enabling board-level interconnect testing between chips without needing bed-of-nails physical probes.
Fig 2.3 — Multiple internal scan chains plus a boundary-scan ring at the chip's I/O
Key Point / Remember

Full scan trades area/power for near-100% fault coverage; partial scan trades some coverage to save area/power. Multiple parallel chains don't change coverage at all — they only reduce test time.

Real-World Example

Full scan is like giving every room in a hotel an inspection checklist; partial scan only inspects the rooms most likely to have problems. Boundary scan is like inspecting only the doors and windows of connected buildings to make sure the wiring between them (not inside them) is fine — exactly how boundary scan checks the connections between chips on a PCB.

Worked Numerical Example — Balancing Scan Chains

Given: A design has 1,000 scan flip-flops, and the DFT engineer wants to split them into 4 balanced parallel scan chains.

Flip-flops per chain = 1000 / 4 = 250

With one single chain of 1,000 cells, shifting in one test pattern takes ~1,000 clock cycles. With 4 balanced chains of 250 cells each (loaded in parallel), the same pattern only takes ~250 clock cycles — a 4× reduction in shift time, directly cutting total test time (see Section 7's numerical for the full calculation).

SECTION 06

Scan Design Rules

Definition

Scan design rules are mandatory guidelines that a circuit must follow before scan insertion, ensuring that the resulting scan chain shifts data cleanly and produces predictable, race-free behavior in both shift and capture modes.

Because every scan cell in a chain shares the same shift path, a single rule violation anywhere can corrupt the entire chain's data — not just one cell.

Key Point / Remember

Think of scan rules as "plumbing rules" for the shift chain: any leak (bus contention), blockage (combinational loop), or mismatched pipe (mixed clock edges) anywhere in the line ruins the flow for the whole chain, not just the local segment.

Real-World Example

It's like a bucket-brigade fire line: if even one person in the middle uses a different-sized bucket, or two people try to pour into the same bucket at once (contention), the whole line's efficiency and accuracy breaks down — not just at that one point.

Table 2.7 — Common Scan Design Rules & Why They Matter
RuleProblem it PreventsTypical Fix
Use only one type of scan flip-flop (same clock edge) per chainMixing positive-edge and negative-edge cells causes race/timing mismatches during shiftInsert a lock-up latch between differently-clocked segments
Avoid combinational feedback loopsFeedback loops can oscillate or hold unknown (X) values, making shift/capture unpredictableBreak the loop by inserting a scan flip-flop, or redesign the logic
Control internal tri-state buses during testTwo drivers can be enabled at once during shift, causing bus contentionAdd test-mode decoding logic so only one driver is active during scan
Isolate/synchronize cross-clock-domain scan cellsCells clocked by different domains can shift data at wrong relative timesAdd a lock-up latch between the two domains' scan chains
Use a dedicated test clock pin, not an internally-gated clockGated/derived clocks may not toggle correctly during shift modeBypass clock-gating logic with a MUX during scan/test mode
SECTION 07

Scan Design Flow

Definition

The scan design flow is the ordered sequence of automated steps an EDA tool follows to convert a plain RTL/gate-level design into a fully scan-testable design, ready for ATPG pattern generation.

Fig 2.4 — The automated scan design flow, RTL to fault simulation

7.1Flow steps explained

  • RTL design: The functional design is written and verified as usual.
  • DFT rule checking: Tools scan the design for scan-rule violations (Section 6) — combinational loops, gated clocks, tri-state contention — and flag them for fixing.
  • Scan cell insertion: Every (or selected) flip-flop is automatically replaced with its scan-cell equivalent (usually Mux-D).
  • Scan stitching: Scan cells are connected in a defined order to form one or more shift chains, with scan-in/scan-out pins routed to the chip boundary.
  • ATPG (Automatic Test Pattern Generation): Test patterns targeting stuck-at (and other) faults are generated, exploiting the scan chain's easy controllability/observability (detailed in Unit 3).
  • Fault simulation: Generated patterns are simulated against fault models to confirm actual fault coverage before patterns are sent to the ATE.
Key Point / Remember

Scan insertion and stitching are almost always done by automated DFT/synthesis tools, not by hand — manual scan insertion for a modern SoC with millions of flip-flops would be practically impossible.

Real-World Example

It's like a factory retrofit: an automated line first checks the blueprint for code violations (rule checking), then robots swap out old machines for new ones with test ports (insertion), then re-route the conveyor belt to link them in sequence (stitching) — all before running any actual test jobs (ATPG/fault sim).

Worked Numerical Example — Scan Test Time Calculation

Given: Scan chain length = 500 cells, number of test patterns = 2,000, clock period = 10 ns. Formula (simplified): total shift cycles ≈ patterns × (chain length + 1).

Single chain (length 500):

Cycles = 2000 × (500 + 1) = 2000 × 501 = 1,002,000 cycles

Test time = 1,002,000 × 10 ns = 10,020,000 ns ≈ 10.02 ms

Now split into 4 balanced chains (length 125 each, from Section 5's numerical):

Cycles = 2000 × (125 + 1) = 2000 × 126 = 252,000 cycles

Test time = 252,000 × 10 ns = 2,520,000 ns ≈ 2.52 ms

Conclusion: Splitting one long chain into 4 parallel chains cuts total test time roughly 4× (from 10.02 ms to 2.52 ms) — directly reducing ATE time and per-chip test cost.

SECTION 08

Special Purpose Scan Designs

Definition

Special purpose scan designs are scan variants engineered to solve a specific limitation of standard serial scan — such as long shift time, high test power, or the need for board-level (not just chip-level) testing.

  • Random Access Scan (RAS): Instead of shifting bit-by-bit through a chain, each scan cell is directly addressable like a memory location, allowing any single cell to be read/written without shifting through all the others.
  • Boundary Scan (IEEE 1149.1 / JTAG): A standardized ring of scan cells placed at every I/O pin, controlled via a Test Access Port (TAP), enabling board-level interconnect testing between chips.
  • Power-aware / segmented scan: Scan chains are shifted in smaller segments rather than all at once, to limit the peak switching activity (and hence power/heat) that a full-chain shift would otherwise cause.
Key Point / Remember

Random access scan trades extra addressing hardware for much lower shift latency and power — it avoids toggling the entire chain just to change one bit, unlike conventional serial scan.

Real-World Example

Conventional serial scan is like passing a note down a full row of students to reach the last one. Random access scan is like a teacher directly calling out a specific student's name to hand them the note — no need to disturb everyone else in the row.

SECTION 09

RTL Design for Testability

Definition

RTL Design for Testability refers to coding guidelines applied while writing synthesizable RTL (Verilog/VHDL) so that the resulting gate-level design is automatically scan-friendly, avoiding costly manual fixes later in the flow.

Fixing a testability problem at the RTL stage is far cheaper than discovering it after synthesis or layout — an echo of the "Rule of Ten" idea from Unit 1, but applied within the design flow itself rather than the manufacturing flow.

Table 2.9 — Common RTL DFT Guidelines
GuidelineProblem It Prevents
Avoid combinational feedback loops in RTLPrevents unresolvable scan-rule violations found later at gate level
Avoid internally-generated (gated) clocks; use clock-enable logic insteadPrevents clocks that fail to toggle correctly during scan shift
Use synchronous resets where possible (or test-mode-controllable async resets)Prevents asynchronous resets from interfering unpredictably with scan capture
Avoid multiplexed/multiple clock edges triggering the same registerPrevents race conditions during scan shift within one domain
Add synchronizers at asynchronous clock-domain boundariesPrevents metastability issues that also break scan chain timing
Avoid internal tri-state buses; use muxes insteadPrevents bus contention during scan shift mode
Key Point / Remember

RTL DFT guidelines exist so that when the automated scan design flow (Section 7) runs, the DFT rule-checking step finds zero or minimal violations — saving redesign cycles.

Real-World Example

It's exactly like including fire-exit doors in a building's blueprint rather than trying to knock new exits into finished concrete walls later. RTL DFT is "testability by blueprint," while ad-hoc post-synthesis fixes are "testability by demolition."

Table 2.10 — Ad-hoc Post-Synthesis Fixes vs. RTL-Level DFT

SECTION 12

5-Mark Questions & Model Answers

Q1. What is testability analysis? Explain SCOAP with its basic controllability rules. 5 Marks
  • Definition: Testability analysis heuristically estimates how hard it is to control/observe circuit nodes, without running full ATPG.
  • SCOAP: Sandia Controllability/Observability Analysis Program — computes CC0, CC1 (combinational controllability to 0/1) and CO (combinational observability) per node.
  • Primary input rule: CC0 = CC1 = 1 for all primary inputs.
  • AND gate: CC1(out) = CC1(a)+CC1(b)+1; CC0(out) = min(CC0(a),CC0(b))+1.
  • Interpretation: Higher values mean harder to control/observe; used to flag weak nodes for ad-hoc test-point insertion.
Q2. Differentiate between ad-hoc DFT and structured DFT (scan-based) approaches. 5 Marks
  • Ad-hoc DFT: Manual, localized fixes at specific hard-to-test nodes identified through testability analysis; not easily automated.
  • Structured DFT: Uniform, systematic technique (scan) applied to every flip-flop; fully automatable by EDA tools.
  • Coverage: Ad-hoc has no formal coverage guarantee; structured DFT gives near-complete, provable fault coverage.
  • Overhead: Ad-hoc adds small, localized overhead; structured DFT adds larger but predictable, uniform overhead (commonly a few percent area).
  • Industry preference: Structured (scan-based) DFT is the default for large SoC designs due to its automation and coverage guarantees.
Q3. Explain the working of a Mux-D scan cell with a diagram. 5 Marks
  • Structure: A 2:1 multiplexer is placed in front of a standard D flip-flop.
  • Inputs to mux: Functional data (D) and scan-in data (SI); selected by the Scan-Enable (SE) control signal.
  • Normal mode (SE=0): Functional data D passes through to the flip-flop as usual.
  • Scan mode (SE=1): Scan-in data SI is loaded instead, so the flip-flop becomes part of a shift-register chain.
  • Advantage: Minimal extra hardware (just one mux per flip-flop), so it is the most widely used scan cell in industry ASIC flows.
Q4. Compare full scan and partial scan architectures. 5 Marks
  • Full scan: Every flip-flop in the design is converted into a scan cell.
  • Partial scan: Only a selected subset of flip-flops (chosen via testability analysis) is converted.
  • Fault coverage: Full scan achieves near-100% coverage; partial scan achieves lower coverage depending on selection quality.
  • Overhead: Full scan has higher area/power/timing overhead; partial scan reduces this overhead by scanning fewer cells.
  • ATPG complexity: Full scan makes ATPG simpler (circuit behaves combinationally for test); partial scan retains some sequential complexity in ATPG.
Q5. List and explain any five scan design rules that must be followed during scan insertion. 5 Marks
  • Single clock-edge type per chain: Avoid mixing positive-edge and negative-edge scan cells in the same chain to prevent race conditions.
  • No combinational feedback loops: These can hold unknown (X) values or oscillate, corrupting shift/capture behavior.
  • Control internal tri-state buses: Add test-mode decoding to ensure only one driver is enabled during scan, avoiding bus contention.
  • Handle cross-clock-domain scan cells: Insert lock-up latches between cells from different clock domains to prevent timing races.
  • Avoid internally-gated clocks in scan mode: Use a dedicated test clock or bypass mux so the clock reliably toggles during shift.
Q6. Describe the scan design flow from RTL to fault simulation. 5 Marks
  • RTL design: Functional design is coded and functionally verified.
  • DFT rule checking: Tools verify the design against scan design rules and flag violations.
  • Scan cell insertion: Flip-flops are automatically replaced with scan-cell equivalents (commonly Mux-D).
  • Scan stitching: Scan cells are connected in order into one or more shift chains, routed to scan-in/out pins.
  • ATPG and fault simulation: Test patterns are automatically generated for target faults, then simulated to confirm actual fault coverage before use on the ATE.
Q7. What is boundary scan? Explain its purpose and structure. 5 Marks
  • Definition: Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1 / JTAG) places scan cells at every I/O pin of a chip, forming a ring around its boundary.
  • Purpose: Tests the interconnections between chips on a printed circuit board without needing physical bed-of-nails probes.
  • Control: Managed by a Test Access Port (TAP) controller, a standardized state machine.
  • Operation: Data can be shifted into boundary cells to drive output pins, or captured from input pins, checking board-level connectivity.
  • Advantage: Standardized across the industry, enabling board test even for densely packed, hard-to-probe modern PCBs.
Q8. Explain the important RTL-level guidelines for design for testability. 5 Marks
  • Avoid combinational feedback loops in RTL code, since they later cause unresolvable scan-rule violations.
  • Avoid internally-generated (gated) clocks; use clock-enable logic instead, so scan clocks toggle reliably.
  • Prefer synchronous resets (or test-mode-controllable async resets) to avoid unpredictable interference during scan capture.
  • Avoid multiple/mixed clock edges triggering the same register within one domain, to prevent shift-time races.
  • Add synchronizers at clock-domain boundaries and avoid internal tri-state buses, preventing metastability and bus-contention issues respectively.
  • Benefit: Fixing these at RTL stage is far cheaper than fixing them after synthesis or layout.

Exam-Oriented Conclusion

Unit 2 takes the testing problem introduced in Unit 1 and answers it constructively: testability can be measured (SCOAP), engineered (ad-hoc or structured DFT), and standardized through scan — from the individual Mux-D or LSSD scan cell, up through full/partial/boundary scan architectures, governed by strict scan design rules, executed via an automated insertion-to-fault-simulation flow, extended by special variants like random access and boundary scan, and ultimately made cheapest to achieve by following DFT guidelines right at the RTL coding stage. Together these nine topics form the practical backbone of modern chip testability, and directly set up Unit 3's logic and fault simulation, where the patterns generated through this scan infrastructure are actually evaluated against fault models.
