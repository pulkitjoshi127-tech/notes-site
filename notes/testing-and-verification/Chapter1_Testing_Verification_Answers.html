Q1What do you mean by Testing of a VLSI chip? Describe importance of Testing. (OR: Justify the role of Testing during VLSI Development Process.)5 Marks

Definition

Testing of a VLSI chip is the process of applying a set of input stimuli (test vectors) to a fabricated circuit and comparing its observed output responses with the expected (fault-free) responses, in order to distinguish good chips from defective ones caused by manufacturing defects such as opens, shorts, bridging, and process variations.

Importance of Testing

  • Quality assurance: Ensures only functionally correct chips reach the customer, improving overall product reliability.
  • Cost control: Defects detected earlier in the flow are far cheaper to fix (Rule of Ten) — testing prevents costly field failures.
  • Yield improvement: Test data helps identify systematic defects in the fabrication process, enabling process correction.
  • Reliability screening: Burn-in and IDDQ tests catch latent/reliability defects before shipment.
  • Diagnosis and failure analysis: Helps locate the physical cause of failure for process improvement.
  • Customer confidence: Guarantees the chip meets datasheet specifications (timing, power, functionality).

Role in VLSI Development

Testing is not a single step but is integrated at every stage — design verification (pre-silicon), wafer probe (post-fabrication), package test, and system-level test — ensuring defects are caught as early as possible before value is added to a faulty part.

Q3Explain Testing for Electronic System Manufacturing Process.5 Marks

Electronic system manufacturing involves several levels of assembly, each requiring its own testing to guarantee that defects are localized and not propagated to costlier subsequent stages.

LevelPurpose of testing
Wafer levelDetects gross fabrication defects before dicing and packaging, saving packaging cost on bad dies
Package/device levelVerifies the packaged IC meets functional and parametric (speed, power) specifications
Board level (PCB)Verifies correct assembly of components (solder joints, opens/shorts) and interconnect integrity
System levelValidates the complete assembled system under real operating conditions
Field/maintenancePeriodic testing to detect degradation during the product's operational life

The guiding principle is to catch a defect at the earliest and cheapest possible stage — this minimizes the total manufacturing cost, since the cost of detecting a fault increases by an order of magnitude at each subsequent level (see Rule of Ten, Q4).

Q4What is the rule of ten in testing? Explain its significance.5 Marks

The Rule of Ten states that the cost of detecting a faulty component/chip increases by roughly 10× at each subsequent stage of the manufacturing/product life cycle if it is not caught at the current stage.

StageRelative cost of fault detection
IC (chip) level1×
Board level10×
System level100×
Field (customer site)1000×

Significance

  • Justifies investment in thorough chip-level testing (DFT, ATPG) as it is far cheaper than fixing issues later.
  • Motivates "test early, test often" philosophy in the design flow.
  • Guides economic trade-off decisions on test coverage versus cost.
  • Encourages Design-for-Testability so faults are exposed at the earliest, cheapest stage.
Q5Explain the concept of "six sigma manufacturing" and its relevance to testing.5 Marks

Six Sigma is a statistical quality-control methodology aimed at reducing process variation so that the manufacturing process produces defects at a rate of no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), i.e., the process spread lies within ±6σ of the mean specification target.

Key Points

  • Higher "sigma level" ⇒ tighter process control ⇒ fewer parametric/process defects.
  • Uses statistical process control (SPC), DMAIC (Define-Measure-Analyze-Improve-Control) methodology.
  • In VLSI, six-sigma control of process parameters (oxide thickness, doping, line width) reduces defect density and improves yield.

Relevance to Testing

  • Even a six-sigma fabrication process cannot guarantee zero defects for chips with billions of transistors — testing remains essential.
  • Six-sigma data helps identify systematic vs random defects, guiding where test effort (fault models, coverage) should focus.
  • Reduces the "defect level" that testing must screen for, thereby lowering test escape rate.

Q7Explain different challenges in VLSI testing.5 Marks
  • Increasing design complexity: Billions of transistors on a chip make exhaustive testing infeasible.
  • Rising test data volume & test time: More gates ⇒ larger test vector sets ⇒ higher ATE memory and time cost.
  • Deep sub-micron effects: Crosstalk, IR-drop, process variation introduce defects not modeled by simple stuck-at faults.
  • Limited controllability/observability: Internal nodes of highly sequential circuits are hard to control/observe from primary I/O.
  • High-speed at-speed testing: Testing timing-related (delay) faults requires ATE operating at functional clock speed.
  • Power dissipation during test: Test mode switching activity can exceed functional power, risking damage.
  • Cost of ATE: High-performance testers are extremely expensive, and test cost is a growing fraction of total product cost.
  • New fault mechanisms: Bridging, opens, timing/delay faults from nanometer processes need new fault models beyond stuck-at.
Q8What is a fault model? Why are fault models necessary in VLSI testing?5 Marks

A fault model is an abstract mathematical/logical representation of the effect that a physical defect (e.g., short, open, particle contamination) has on circuit behaviour. Instead of dealing with countless physical defect mechanisms, the fault is mapped to a simple logical fault (e.g., stuck-at-0/1, bridging, delay fault) at the gate/transistor level.

Why fault models are necessary

  • Physical defects are too numerous and technology-dependent to enumerate individually — fault models generalize them.
  • They provide a computationally tractable target for ATPG algorithms.
  • Allow quantitative measurement of test quality via fault coverage.
  • Enable fault simulation to predict defect-detection capability of a test set before fabrication.
  • Provide a common language between test engineers across different technologies.

Common Fault Models

  • Stuck-at fault model (most widely used)
  • Bridging fault model
  • Transistor (stuck-open/stuck-short) fault model
  • Delay fault models (gate-delay, path-delay, transition)
Q9What is fault collapsing? How is it useful in testing?5 Marks

Fault collapsing is the technique of reducing the total fault list of a circuit by grouping faults that are equivalent or dominant, so that a smaller "collapsed" fault set needs to be targeted while still guaranteeing complete detection of the original fault list.

Types

  • Equivalence fault collapsing: Two faults are equivalent if they produce identical faulty output for every input pattern — any one representative fault from the equivalent class need be tested.
  • Dominance fault collapsing: Fault A dominates fault B if every test that detects B also detects A — then A can be dropped from the fault list (only the dominated fault B needs to remain, as B is harder to detect).

Usefulness

  • Reduces the number of faults an ATPG tool must target, hence reduces CPU/runtime.
  • Reduces test generation and fault-simulation time without losing fault coverage.
  • Simplifies fault reporting and diagnosis.

Example: For an AND gate, an input stuck-at-0 fault is equivalent to output stuck-at-0, so only one representative fault needs testing.

Q10What do you understand by Transistor Faults in CMOS circuits? How can we detect transistor faults? Take 2-input CMOS NAND gate as an example.5 Marks

Transistor faults occur when a MOS transistor fails to switch correctly due to physical defects — classified as:

  • Stuck-open fault: A transistor that should turn ON remains permanently OFF (open circuit), causing the output node to float (retain previous value) instead of charging/discharging.
  • Stuck-short fault: A transistor that should be OFF remains permanently ON, creating an unwanted conducting path (often causing contention between pull-up and pull-down networks).
Fig: 2-input CMOS NAND — PA,PB (PMOS pull-up, parallel) and NA,NB (NMOS pull-down, series)

Detection Method

Faulty transistorEffectDetecting input sequence
NA stuck-openOutput floats when A=B=1 is applied after a prior state where Q was highTwo-pattern test: (A=0 or B=0) → (A=1,B=1); output fails to go low
PA stuck-shortContention when A=1,B=1 — pull-up PA conducts along with pull-down path, causing incorrect/weak logic level or excess currentDetected via IDDQ (abnormal quiescent current) test

Transistor stuck-open faults require a two-pattern (sequence) test — an initialization pattern followed by the test pattern — because the fault effect depends on the previous stored charge on the output node, unlike combinational stuck-at faults.

Q11What is IDDQ testing? What are its advantages and limitations?5 Marks

IDDQ testing measures the steady-state (quiescent) supply current IDDQ drawn by a CMOS circuit when it is in a stable logic state (no switching activity). In a defect-free CMOS circuit this current should be extremely small (only leakage), since in steady state either the pull-up or pull-down network is OFF. A defect (e.g., bridging fault, gate-oxide short, stuck-short transistor) creates an unwanted conduction path, causing an abnormally high IDDQ, which flags a faulty chip.

Advantages

  • Detects defects (bridging, gate-oxide shorts) that logic (stuck-at) testing may miss.
  • Simple pass/fail measurement — does not need to sensitize/propagate a logic fault to an output.
  • Effective at catching reliability-related latent defects, improving long-term quality.
  • Requires relatively few test vectors compared to full logic ATPG.

Limitations

  • Test application time is longer since the circuit must settle before current is measured.
  • Background leakage current in deep sub-micron technologies is increasing, reducing the fault-to-leakage current ratio and making faults harder to distinguish.
  • Cannot detect faults that do not cause excess quiescent current (e.g., pure stuck-open with no static path).
  • Requires precise, low-noise current measurement equipment — increases ATE cost.

Q13Explain Clocked scan cell with necessary waveform.5 Marks

A clocked scan cell is a scan flip-flop built using master-slave latches, where the scan data input is loaded through a dedicated clock (SCK), separate from the system clock, avoiding the need for a mux at the D input (unlike Muxed-D). It uses 4 non-overlapping clocks: system clock (CK), scan clock A (master), scan clock B, and hold clock.

Fig: Non-overlapping clock waveforms of a clocked scan cell

Operation

  • Shift mode: SCK-A and SCK-B pulse alternately to shift scan data serially through master-slave latches.
  • Capture mode: System clock CK captures functional data into the cell during normal operation.
  • Non-overlapping clocking avoids race conditions between shift and system clock domains.

Advantage: No extra mux delay in the data path (better speed than Muxed-D). Disadvantage: Requires additional clock signals/routing, increasing design complexity.

Q14Why is it difficult to test sequential circuit? How does scan design concept help solve this problem?5 Marks

Difficulty in testing sequential circuits

  • Poor controllability: Internal state (flip-flop) values cannot be directly set from primary inputs; require a sequence of clock cycles to reach a desired state.
  • Poor observability: Internal states cannot be directly observed at primary outputs; must be shifted out through several clock cycles.
  • State space explosion: Number of states grows exponentially with number of flip-flops (2n), making exhaustive testing impractical.
  • Sequential ATPG complexity: Generating test sequences requires tracing back through multiple time frames, which is computationally far more expensive than combinational ATPG.
  • Initialization problem: Some states may not be reachable/resettable, complicating test sequence generation.

How Scan Design Helps

  • Scan design connects all (or most) flip-flops into a shift register (scan chain) during test mode.
  • Provides direct controllability: desired states can be shifted in serially.
  • Provides direct observability: internal flip-flop contents can be shifted out and examined.
  • Converts the sequential ATPG problem into a much simpler combinational ATPG problem, since flip-flops effectively become pseudo primary inputs/outputs.
  • Drastically reduces test generation time and improves achievable fault coverage.
Q15Write down main decisions that are made at scan configuration stage in scan design flow.5 Marks

Scan configuration is the stage of the scan design flow where the overall scan architecture is planned before insertion. Key decisions include:

  • Scan architecture selection: Full-scan, partial-scan, or random-access scan.
  • Scan cell type selection: Muxed-D, Clocked-scan, or LSSD scan cell.
  • Number of scan chains: Deciding how many parallel scan chains to use, based on available I/O pins and desired shift-time reduction.
  • Scan chain ordering: Deciding the physical/logical order in which flip-flops are connected in a chain to minimize routing overhead.
  • Balancing chain lengths: Distributing flip-flops evenly among chains to minimize shift time.
  • Clock and reset strategy: Handling multiple clock domains, asynchronous set/reset signals during scan shift.
  • Test pin/port allocation: Assigning scan-in, scan-out, scan-enable pins.
  • Power considerations during shift: Deciding shift-clock frequency and test power management strategy.

Q17What do you understand by stuck-at faults? How can we detect stuck-at faults? Take 2-to-1 multiplexer circuit as example.5 Marks

A stuck-at fault models a manufacturing defect as a signal line permanently fixed at logic 0 (stuck-at-0, SA0) or logic 1 (stuck-at-1, SA1), independent of the actual signal that should be driving that line. It is the most widely used fault model due to its simplicity and reasonable correlation to real defects.

Fig: 2:1 MUX — fault site marked on line A

Detection

FaultDetecting test (S,A,B)Reasoning
A s-a-0S=0, A=1, B=don't careExpected Y=1, faulty Y=0 (mismatch reveals fault)
A s-a-1S=0, A=0Expected Y=0, faulty Y=1

To detect a stuck-at fault: (1) Activate the fault by setting the faulty line to the opposite of the stuck value, and (2) Propagate the resulting logic difference to a primary output where it can be observed.

Q18What is the similarity between open fault with stuck-at and transistor faults? Discuss bridging fault and different types of behaviour shown by it.5 Marks

Similarity

An open fault on a wire/transistor causes a node to lose its driving path, similar to how a transistor stuck-open fault leaves the output floating — both can, under certain conditions, be modeled as an equivalent stuck-at fault (the node retains its previous logic value, mimicking a stuck condition for a single test but requiring two-pattern tests to fully expose, since it depends on circuit state/memory effect rather than being permanently fixed like a pure stuck-at fault).

Bridging Faults

A bridging fault is an unintended short between two normally independent signal lines caused by a manufacturing defect (e.g., metal bridge, via short).

Behaviour typeDescription
Wired-AND bridgingResulting shorted node takes logic AND of the two bridged signals (dominant pull-down)
Wired-OR bridgingResulting shorted node takes logic OR of the two bridged signals (dominant pull-up)
Dominant bridgingOne signal's driver is stronger and forces its value onto the other line
Feedback bridgingBridge creates an unintended feedback path, potentially converting combinational logic into a sequential (oscillatory/latching) circuit

The 2:1 MUX has 4 signal lines (A, B, S, Y). Under single-fault (stuck-at) model, each line can have SA0 and SA1 ⇒ 4 lines × 2 = 8 single stuck-at faults (before collapsing).

For n = 4: 34 − 1 = 80 possible multiple-fault combinations

(Each line can be: fault-free, SA0, or SA1 = 3 states; subtract the 1 all-fault-free combination.) This illustrates why multiple-fault models are computationally far less tractable than the single-fault model.

Q20What is bridging fault? Explain various bridging fault models.5 Marks

A bridging fault is a defect that creates an unintended low-resistance electrical connection (short) between two signal lines that are otherwise meant to be electrically independent, typically caused by a metal bridge, via short, or contamination during fabrication.

ModelDescription
Wired-AND modelThe bridged net assumes the logical AND of the two shorted signals (models the case where the pull-down network is stronger)
Wired-OR modelThe bridged net assumes the logical OR of the two shorted signals (pull-up network stronger)
Dominant-AND / Dominant-OR (Indeterminate)One driver dominates depending on relative driving strength/technology; used when neither pure AND/OR model applies exactly
Feedback bridging faultThe short creates a feedback loop, potentially converting a combinational circuit into a sequential one with new states/oscillation
Non-feedback bridging faultThe short does not create a loop; circuit remains combinational but with modified logic behaviour

Bridging faults are typically detected using IDDQ testing (since a short causes excess quiescent current) as well as by logic tests that force the two bridged nodes to opposite values, thereby creating a detectable output difference under the wired-AND/OR assumption.

Q22Describe Crosstalk and its types.5 Marks

Crosstalk is unwanted electrical interference between two adjacent signal wires (interconnects) caused by coupling capacitance (and to a lesser extent inductance) between them. As feature sizes shrink and wire spacing decreases, coupling capacitance becomes comparable to or exceeds the wire's own ground capacitance, making crosstalk a significant deep sub-micron effect.

Types

TypeDescription
Crosstalk GlitchA transition on an aggressor line induces a spurious voltage glitch (pulse) on a quiet (static) victim line, potentially causing false logic switching
Crosstalk DelaySimultaneous switching of an aggressor and victim line (same or opposite direction) speeds up or slows down the victim's signal transition, causing timing faults

Crosstalk becomes a major reliability and testing concern in nanometer technology nodes, requiring new fault models (crosstalk fault models) beyond the classical stuck-at model.

Q23What is crosstalk? Explain crosstalk glitches and crosstalk delays.5 Marks

Crosstalk is signal interference between adjacent interconnect lines caused by parasitic coupling capacitance (Cc) between them, which becomes dominant relative to the line-to-ground capacitance in deep sub-micron VLSI designs.

Fig: Aggressor transition coupling a glitch onto a quiet victim line

Crosstalk Glitch

Occurs when the victim line is in a steady (non-switching) state and an adjacent aggressor line switches — the coupling capacitor injects charge/discharge current onto the victim, producing a voltage glitch that may be misinterpreted as a valid logic transition by a downstream gate, causing functional failure.

Crosstalk Delay

Occurs when both aggressor and victim switch simultaneously. If they switch in opposite directions, the effective coupling capacitance doubles (Miller effect), slowing the victim's transition (delay fault); if they switch in the same direction, the transition speeds up. This affects setup/hold timing and can cause the circuit to fail at rated speed — requiring at-speed delay testing to detect.

Q26What are the features of ATE?5 Marks

ATE (Automatic Test Equipment) is specialized hardware used to apply test vectors to a chip/board and evaluate its responses automatically, without manual intervention.

  • Pattern generator: Stores and applies test vectors/sequences to the device under test (DUT) at specified timing.
  • Timing generator: Produces precise clock and strobe signals for at-speed and functional testing.
  • Pin electronics / driver-comparator: Drives input pins and compares DUT output against expected values with configurable voltage levels.
  • Power supply unit: Provides and monitors DUT power (voltage/current) including parametric (IDDQ) measurement capability.
  • DUT interface board (Load board): Custom interface connecting the ATE to the specific device package/socket.
  • Failure/error memory: Logs failing vectors and pin locations for diagnosis.
  • High parallelism/multi-site testing: Ability to test multiple chips simultaneously to increase throughput.
  • High-speed operation: Capable of applying test patterns at (or beyond) the device's rated operating frequency for at-speed/delay fault testing.
  • Software/control system: User interface for test program development, data logging, and yield analysis.
Q27Explain the boundary scan architecture (IEEE 1149.1) with a neat diagram.5 Marks

Boundary Scan (IEEE 1149.1, JTAG) is a standardized DFT architecture that places a scan cell (Boundary Scan Cell, BSC) at every I/O pin of a chip, allowing testing of chip-to-chip interconnects on a board and internal core logic through a standard 4/5-pin Test Access Port (TAP), without needing physical probe access.

Fig: Boundary scan cells at every I/O pin, controlled by TAP

Key Components

  • Boundary Scan Cell (BSC): Placed at each I/O pin; can capture pin value, shift data serially, and update/drive the pin — forming a chain around the chip boundary.
  • TAP (Test Access Port): Standard 4 pins — TDI (Test Data In), TDO (Test Data Out), TCK (Test Clock), TMS (Test Mode Select), and optional TRST (Reset).
  • TAP Controller: A 16-state finite state machine controlled by TMS that governs shift, capture, and update operations.
  • Instruction Register (IR) and Data Registers (DR): IR selects operation mode (e.g., EXTEST, BYPASS, SAMPLE/PRELOAD); DR includes the boundary-scan register and bypass register.

Applications

Testing board-level interconnects (open/short between chips) without physical probes, in-system programming, and testing internal core logic via EXTEST/INTEST instructions.

Q28Explain the D-algorithm in automatic test pattern generation.5 Marks

The D-algorithm (developed by J. P. Roth) is a classic path-oriented ATPG algorithm used to generate a test for a targeted stuck-at fault by using a special symbol D (representing 1 in fault-free circuit, 0 in faulty circuit) and its complement D̄ (0 in fault-free, 1 in faulty) to track the fault signal through the circuit.

Key Steps

  1. Fault Activation (Excitation): Set the faulty line to the value opposite of the stuck-at value, generating a D (or D̄) at the fault site.
  2. D-Drive (Fault Propagation): Propagate the D/D̄ signal from the fault site towards a primary output by choosing appropriate values on the side inputs of gates along the path (using the D-cube of the gate's primitive).
  3. Consistency / Line Justification: Justify all the required internal signal values back to the primary inputs, ensuring no conflicting assignments (using singular cover / justification).
  4. Backtracking: If a conflict arises, the algorithm backtracks to try alternative propagation paths or justification choices.
SymbolMeaning
DGood circuit = 1, Faulty circuit = 0
D̄ (D')Good circuit = 0, Faulty circuit = 1

The D-algorithm was historically significant as the first complete algorithm capable of generating a test for any detectable combinational stuck-at fault, forming the foundation for later, more efficient algorithms (PODEM, FAN).

Q29The number of failures in 109 hours (FITS) is used in reliability calculations. Calculate MTBF for a system with 500 components, each having a failure rate of 1000 FITS.5 Marks

Given

  • Number of components, N = 500
  • Failure rate per component = 1000 FITS = 1000 failures per 109 hours

Solution

λ (per component) = 1000 / 109 hours = 1 × 10−6 failures/hour

Assuming components are in series (system fails if any one component fails), total system failure rate:
λsystem = N × λ = 500 × 1 × 10−6 = 5 × 10−4 failures/hour

MTBF = 1 / λsystem = 1 / (5 × 10−4) = 2000 hours

Result: MTBF = 2000 hours (≈ 83.3 days) for the 500-component system, assuming exponential failure law and series (non-redundant) configuration where failure of any single component causes system failure.
